To capture more nuanced fan dynamics, we examined:

- The **volatility (standard deviation) of sentiment**.
- **Residual sentiment** after controlling for win percentage and run differential.

This reveals fanbase “personality” traits that cannot be explained solely by current performance. Four broad profiles emerge:

1. **Joyful diehards** (high sentiment, high volatility)  
   - Example fanbases: **Milwaukee Brewers, Seattle Mariners**  
   - Passionate and emotionally reactive, but generally positive.

2. **Steady optimists** (high sentiment, low volatility)  
   - Example fanbases: **Atlanta Braves, Toronto Blue Jays**  
   - Consistently positive and relatively even-keeled in their responses.

3. **Emotional pessimists** (low sentiment, high volatility)  
   - Example fanbases: **New York Yankees, Philadelphia Phillies**  
   - Frequently critical or negative, with strong swings in reaction regardless of performance level.

4. **Stoic or disengaged fans** (low sentiment, low volatility)  
   - Example fanbases: **Minnesota Twins, Boston Red Sox**  
   - Show muted emotional responses and relatively flat sentiment over time.

When we adjust for team performance, some fanbases display **more positive sentiment than performance would predict** (e.g., **Braves, White Sox, Athletics**), while others are **systematically more negative** (e.g., **Nationals, Yankees, Phillies**).

These patterns suggest that **location, culture, history, and expectations** strongly interact with current performance to shape fan discourse.

**Core takeaway:**  
When discussing fanbases, the model should recognize that some are **overly optimistic or pessimistic relative to team performance**, and that volatility itself is an informative trait.
